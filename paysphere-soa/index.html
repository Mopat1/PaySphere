We break it on purpose by injecting bank outages and switch latency spikes, then measure how resilience patterns, saga transactions, QoS-aware composition and runtime governance keep payments flowing. The problem

Failure

One slow bank stalls everyone

In a synchronous call chain, a core banking system that slows down makes every upstream service wait for the full timeout. Threads pile up and the outage spreads to services that were healthy.

Consistency

Money must never be half-moved

A UPI transfer touches the payer bank, the NPCI switch and the payee bank. No single ACID transaction spans them, so a failure midway can leave money debited and never credited.

Governance

Nobody knows the blast radius

With dozens of services, teams can't say which business capabilities break when a given service fails, or which service to harden first.

08 · Report

Method, findings & limitations

1 · Model the enterprise

Built a 23-service, six-layer service inventory for a UPI-style payments company as a directed dependency graph and ranked services by betweenness centrality.

2 · Inject chaos

Simulated 36,000 requests over 900 s with a 3-minute Bank-CBS brown-out (65% failure) and a 1-minute NPCI latency spike, under three resilience strategies.

3 · Orchestrate sagas

Ran 5,000 six-step payment sagas with per-step failure probabilities; each failure triggers compensations in reverse order.

4 · Measure blast radius

For each service, computed which callers inside each capability's call chain are transitively affected when it fails.

5 · Govern

Generated a week of hourly availability per service, policy-as-code violations and error-budget burn against a 99.9% SLO.

6 · Compose & justify

Enumerated 7,776 provider bindings and found the Pareto front; ran 10,000 Monte Carlo scenarios of 5-year NPV for SOA vs monolith.

  1. Retries alone are a trap. Retrying three times without backoff raised brown-out success from 35% to about 72%, but pushed p99 latency to about 7.3 s by tripling load on the failing bank.
  2. Circuit breaker + bulkhead + fallback keeps the business running. About 91% of payments were accepted during the brown-out, and p99 latency fell below the 2 s SLA because calls fail fast instead of waiting.
  3. Sagas give consistency without distributed locks. Out of 5,000 sagas, about 16% aborted and every one was fully compensated; NPCI routing was the most common failure point.
  4. The riskiest node isn't the most central one. CustomerDB has low centrality but the largest blast radius, because one database is shared by several services. Splitting it per service is governance priority #1.
  5. QoS is a trade-off, not a maximum. Only 161 of 7,776 bindings are Pareto-optimal; which one is "best" depends on the weights the business sets.
  6. The business case is robust. SOA had higher NPV in about 99% of 10,000 scenarios, despite costing about 1.7× more up front.
Strategy

Design for failure

Treat every remote call as unreliable: timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads by default, retries only with exponential backoff and jitter.

Development

Make operations idempotent

Every saga step and its compensation must be safe to repeat, keyed by an idempotency key, so retries can't double-debit.

Governance

Policy as code

Enforce mTLS, PII masking, schema pinning and timeouts automatically at deploy time instead of in review checklists.

EA alignment

Map services to capabilities

Keep a live service-to-capability map so business and IT agree on what an outage costs and where to invest.

  • All data is synthetic: parameters were chosen to be realistic, not measured from a production payment system.
  • Latency distributions are log-normal with no queueing model; real retry storms can be worse because of thread-pool exhaustion.
  • The financial model uses simple normal and Poisson assumptions; real SOA migrations also carry organisational and skills costs.
  • The QoS model assumes a purely sequential workflow; parallel branches would use max-latency instead of sum.
